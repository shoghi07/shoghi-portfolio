Case study · Delicut · UAE meal subscription · 2025 · 11 min read

Less friction, without less confidence.

Rethinking how people choose, buy and manage a meal subscription, from the first visit to the weeks after checkout.

I led UX strategy and product direction for Delicut (opens in a new tab), a UAE meal-subscription service, working day to day with the founder and two designers. The redesigned landing page, checkout and mobile app all shipped.

The journey

A subscription is not one moment. It’s six.

Delicut already had paying customers. I worked across their whole journey, from first visit to the weeks after purchase, rather than on a single screen.

Research · 5 conversations, CX, recordings

Five questions people needed answered before committing.

I spoke with renewers, people who didn’t renew, and visitors who left, and added CX input, analytics and session recordings. A small, directional sample: recordings showed where people left, not why. Some found the site clean and easy; the gaps were in what it answered.

  1. Can I see the actual meals? Menu transparency → Landing page
  2. Does it fit my goals? Some didn’t know customization existed until CX told them → Plan builder
  3. Can I trust it? Reviews, freshness, quality → Landing page
  4. Is it worth the price? Weighed against cooking and ordering in → Landing · checkout
  5. What if my week changes? Skipping, pausing, trial length → Mobile app

The insight

Minimize process friction — not decision confidence.

Removing steps could make the process lighter. Removing information too early would leave people unsure what they were buying. So the goal was never “fewer steps”.

Discover + Decide · Landing page Shipped · live 1+ yr

Help people decide before asking them to subscribe.

Evidence
People wanted real meals, fit with their goals, value against cooking or ordering in, and trust. Stakeholders had assumed price wouldn’t matter much; research said it did.
Decision
I restructured the page around the questions people asked, and pushed hardest for real meals and the weekly menu near the top.
Status
Shipped. Three iterations, V1 live, then V2. Live 1+ year; roughly 80–90% of the design remains (my estimate). Conversion wasn’t measured.

So the meals came first.

Six dishes from Delicut’s current menu, from Mandi and Machboos to biryani and bowls. Real food is what the page puts near the top.

The content was already there. It was in the wrong order for the questions people were asking.

The original page had a meal-plan proposition, benefits, a menu preview, pricing and testimonials, arranged as product → features → offer. I reordered it into a confidence sequence and added goals, so people could recognize themselves before choosing a plan.

Build · Plan builder Partly shipped

Don’t make everyone start from the same place.

Evidence
Customization and goals came up again and again, and some people didn’t know customization existed. Plans also assumed a single person.
Decision
One plan builder and one checkout; only the starting point varies. Goal-first came first, because most customers weren’t nutrition experts. Their goal pre-selects a calorie range they can still change.
Status
Goal-first went live during the engagement. We explored calorie-first and Flexi as concepts; neither was validated.

People didn’t arrive knowing the same things.

Some knew a goal, like losing weight, but not a calorie target. Some already knew their exact numbers. Some needed a plan that bent around a changing week. Many just wanted to pick meals and go. I mapped each to an entry point that matched how they think, and brought every route to the same finish line.

  • StandardCoreI know what I want. Let me build it.
  • Goal-firstShippedI know my goal, not my numbers.
  • Calorie-firstExploredI already track my targets.
  • FlexiExploredMy week, and my household, change.

Buy · Subscription checkout Shipped · V2

Make subscribing feel like buying.

Evidence
Observed: recordings showed where people left, and stakeholders flagged drop-off at mobile-number (OTP) verification. Not established: why. V1 improved the flow but kept its sign-up shape.
Decision
I reframed it as an order: configure, see the cost, give delivery details, pay. A design hypothesis, not a proven fix.
Status
Shipped — V2 is live. Conversion, completion and order value weren’t measured. AED values in the screens are examples.
  1. 1 · Customize planGoal or diet, meals, calories, exclusions, days, duration, start date.
  2. 2 · DeliveryContact asked for as what delivery updates need: phone or email. Then address, slot, payment.
  3. 3 · ConfirmationThe end of the purchase, and a hand-off to the app.
  4. Order summary visible at every step: plan · delivery fee · VAT · discount · total
The trade-off

A phone-only start would have been faster. I kept delivery and preferences before payment, because people wanted to understand meals, fit and delivery before committing.

Manage · Mobile app Shipped · v1.0 live, v2.0 in production

The purchase was only the beginning.

Evidence
There was no app. On the web, customers could already skip or pause, but pausing was too coarse: there was no way to skip a few specific days inside a plan, and meals couldn’t be changed. Every change needed 48 hours’ notice.
Decision
I made the app the home of the ongoing relationship, built around Manage Deliveries: one calendar for skipping and resuming individual days. We explored a Pro tier on top.
Status
v1.0 live; v2.0, a visual and UX refinement, in production. Support load, retention and engagement weren’t measured.

Adapt · where it could go Explored · not shipped

From delivering meals to adapting around the person.

The founder wanted Delicut to become more than meal delivery, and a wellness survey pointed to interest in coaching and tracking (directional, not proof of demand). I turned that into concepts: a Pro tier that logs activity and outside meals and adjusts future meals, and an AI Coach. We also sketched trainer and corporate-wellness tools. None were built or validated.

Learnings

What Delicut taught me

I joined to help with research and grew into leading the product experience. I learned as much from the founder and the team as I brought to them. These are the lessons I’ve carried into my work since.

  1. 01 · Partnership

    Good product decisions came from working closely with the founder.

    Working with the founder every day, I helped weigh priorities, shape the roadmap and decide what could wait. It was the part of the project I enjoyed most.

    What I carry forwardI try to join product conversations early, so design can help shape priorities, not only respond to them.

  2. 02 · Shared understanding

    Explaining the “why” helped the team move past copying layouts.

    On the landing page, it was tempting to point at another site and copy its layout. I worked with the team to give each section a clear objective: which persona it serves and which assumption it tests. Reviewing Hotjar heatmaps and recordings together made those conversations concrete.

    What I carry forwardI give every section a purpose the team can question, and use real behaviour to ground the discussion.

  3. 03 · Leading a small team

    Clear briefs and close reviews kept two designers moving in one direction.

    I led the client conversations and the UX; our visual designer owned the landing page and visual style, and our UI designer the mobile app. With every brief, I explained the product strategy behind the task and reviewed the work closely, and a design system we set up early kept the UI consistent.

    What I carry forwardI invest in the brief and its context up front, so designers can make good calls without lots of rework.

  4. 04 · Bringing others along

    Familiar patterns made new ideas easier to agree on.

    For managing deliveries, we borrowed the date-picking model people know from booking apps like Airbnb: a calendar where each day changes with one tap. Starting from something familiar made it easy for the founder to say yes, and it became one of the app’s most valuable improvements.

    What I carry forwardWhen I ask stakeholders to commit to something new, I start from a pattern they already know.

  5. 05 · Designing for the business

    A design is only as good as the team’s ability to run it.

    Promotions, payments and upsells needed sign-off from marketing, finance, accounts and sales, and operations needed 48 hours’ notice for any change. Some of our prototyped animations were also hard for developers to rebuild.

    What I carry forwardI bring operations, finance and engineering in early, and document motion clearly when it matters.

  6. What I’d do differently

    Ship the core first, then build towards the vision.

    I’d still spend time imagining what the app could become, but keep it separate from what had to ship. On the web, I’d keep the same order: subscription flow first. For the app, I’d agree early on what Pro and Non-Pro include, launch Non-Pro straight away, and introduce Pro a month or two later. It would have saved time and followed how people actually adopt the product.

Understand the decision. Then design the relationship.

What I took from Delicut: a purchase flow is part of a larger confidence-building journey, a shorter process isn’t better if it makes the offer harder to judge, and a subscription needs designing for the weeks after checkout.
